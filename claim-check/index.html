How this works, and what it can't do

Everything runs in this page with plain JavaScript. There is no AI model, no API and no internet connection. The browser is told to block all network access, so you can paste confidential figures. Instead of asking another AI whether the answer is right, the tool checks the parts of an answer that can be checked mechanically. Those are the parts most often wrong in convincing AI writing.

1. Does the maths add up?

It recalculates percentage changes, margins (gross, EBITDA, operating, net), leverage, current ratio, interest cover, CAGR, "X of Y" shares, totals, and splits that should add up to 100%. It allows for rounding, so £12.4m vs £12,437,000 isn't flagged.

2. Is it consistent with itself?

It catches the same metric quoted with two different values, a "rise" where the numbers fall, a "5-year period" from 2019 to 2022, a weekday that doesn't match the date, impossible dates such as 30 February, and future events described in the past tense.

3. Does it match your source? (tab 2)

Every figure is traced back to the source. If it isn't there, the tool tries to calculate it from any two source figures (sum, difference, ratio, % change). A figure that is neither found nor derivable is flagged as unsupported. Names that aren't in the source and statements that flip the source's direction (rise vs fall, profit vs loss) are flagged too.

4. Does it say the same thing twice? (tab 3)

This is the "self-consistency" method used in hallucination research. If you ask the same question several times, invented details vary between answers and real ones stay put. Any figure that appears in only one answer, or that another answer contradicts, is flagged.

5. Built-in expert knowledge

It knows which IFRS / IAS / FRS / ISA standards exist and which have been withdrawn. Citing IAS 17 for leases or IAS 18 for revenue is a classic AI mistake. It also knows the Companies Act 2006 section range. It flags references a person must check by hand: URLs, DOIs, studies, surveys, "according to McKinsey", case law, legislation. It also flags vague authority ("studies show…"), unsourced benchmarks and absolute claims ("always", "guaranteed").

What it can't do

  • It can't tell whether a claim about the outside world is true, for example whether a Deloitte survey actually said 68%. It flags those for a person to check instead.
  • No flags doesn't mean the answer is correct. It means nothing checkable was found to be wrong. Judgement calls and advice still need an expert.
  • Without tab 2 or tab 3 the tool can only check internal maths, logic and references. Adding a source turns it into a full fact-check of every figure.

Free extra step: make the AI show its work

Paste this into the same chat after the answer, then paste its reply into tab 3 or check it the same way:

For every factual claim and figure in your previous answer, list:
1) the claim, 2) where it came from (the document/data I gave you, a named and dated public source, or "my general knowledge"), 3) any calculation, shown step by step, 4) your confidence (high/medium/low).
If you cannot point to a source, say so plainly. Do not invent sources, links or citations.
