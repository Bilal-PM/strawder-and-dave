How this works, and what it can't do

Everything runs in this page with plain JavaScript. There is no AI model, no API and no internet connection. The browser is told to block all network access, so you can paste confidential figures. Instead of asking another AI whether the answer is right, the tool checks the parts of an answer that can be checked mechanically. Those are the parts most often wrong in convincing AI writing.

1. Does the maths add up?

It recalculates percentage changes, margins (gross, EBITDA, operating, net), leverage, current ratio, interest cover, CAGR, "X of Y" shares, totals, and splits that should add up to 100%. It allows for rounding, so £12.4m vs £12,437,000 isn't flagged.

2. Is it consistent with itself?

It catches the same metric quoted with two different values, a "rise" where the numbers fall, a "5-year period" from 2019 to 2022, a weekday that doesn't match the date, impossible dates such as 30 February, and future events described in the past tense.

Legal checks: what legal AI tools worry about most

Studies of legal AI tools find the most damaging errors aren't made-up facts. They are real-looking material that doesn't say what's claimed: altered quotations, citations to provisions that don't exist, the right clause number on the wrong point, and "may" quietly becoming "must". With your document in tab 2, this tool checks that every quotation appears word-for-word (it shows exactly which words changed), that every cited clause, section, schedule or paragraph exists, and that what the answer says matches the text of the clause it cites. It also compares obligation strength (may / must / must not) against the source. Without a source it still checks words against figures ("thirty (45) days"), recalculates deadlines (calendar and business days) and tests case citations for impossibilities: a UK Supreme Court citation before 2009, a House of Lords citation after 2009, King's Bench before 2022, or a US reporter series cited outside its years.

3. Does it match your source? (tab 2)

Every figure is traced back to the source. If it isn't there, the tool tries to calculate it from any two source figures (sum, difference, ratio, % change). A figure that is neither found nor derivable is flagged as unsupported. Names that aren't in the source and statements that flip the source's direction (rise vs fall, profit vs loss) are flagged too.

4. Does it say the same thing twice? (tab 3)

This is the "self-consistency" method used in hallucination research. If you ask the same question several times, invented details vary between answers and real ones stay put. Any figure that appears in only one answer, or that another answer contradicts, is flagged.

5. Built-in expert knowledge

It knows which IFRS / IAS / FRS / ISA standards exist and which have been withdrawn. Citing IAS 17 for leases or IAS 18 for revenue is a classic AI mistake. It also knows the Companies Act 2006 section range. It flags references a person must check by hand: URLs, DOIs, studies, surveys, "according to McKinsey", case law, legislation. It also flags vague authority ("studies show…"), unsourced benchmarks and absolute claims ("always", "guaranteed").

What it can't do

  • It can't tell whether a claim about the outside world is true, for example whether a Deloitte survey actually said 68%. It flags those for a person to check instead.
  • No flags doesn't mean the answer is correct. It means nothing checkable was found to be wrong. Judgement calls and advice still need an expert.
  • Without tab 2 or tab 3 the tool can only check internal maths, logic and references. Adding a source turns it into a full fact-check of every figure.

Free extra step: make the AI check itself

This prompt uses the "chain of verification" technique from hallucination research. Open a new chat so the AI can't simply defend its earlier answer, paste the prompt, then check the reply here too.

You are checking a draft written by someone else. Do not assume it is correct.
1. Break the draft into separate factual claims (one fact per line, no pronouns).
2. For each claim, write one question that would prove or disprove it.
3. Answer each question on its own, without looking at the draft's wording.
4. Re-do every calculation step by step.
5. Give each claim a verdict: SUPPORTED, CONTRADICTED or CANNOT VERIFY, with the source you relied on (a document I provided, or a named, dated public source). If you have no source, say CANNOT VERIFY. Never invent sources, links or citations.
6. Finish with a corrected version of the draft.

DRAFT:

Why it won't go out of date

The checks don't depend on any particular AI model, version or provider. Arithmetic, internal consistency, tracing figures to your source and comparing answers work the same on any AI's output, today or in ten years. The parts that can age, such as which standards exist or which rates and terms have been replaced, are plain lists. Rules with effective dates switch on by themselves using today's date, and you can add your own in the "Your rules" tab without touching code.

What this learned from (ideas only, no code copied)

  • Self-consistency: a model is less consistent about facts it is inventing. First shown model-free by SelfCheckGPT (2023), and still the basis of current methods such as semantic entropy (Nature, 2024) and UQLM (2026). Tab 3 uses this principle with its own text matching, so it doesn't depend on any one paper's code.
  • FActScore and Loki / OpenFactVerification: split an answer into claims, score only the claims that can be decided, and never count "can't verify" as true. That is the claim-by-claim table.
  • factool: re-compute every written-out calculation.
  • RefChecker (Amazon): a figure can be real but attached to the wrong subject. Here, a source number must match the right metric, not just appear somewhere.
  • ClaimBuster (political debate claim spotting) and Full Fact's statistical-claim spotter: which sentences are worth checking, and ignoring label numbers like "Article 37".
  • LettuceDetect, MiniCheck, Vectara HHEM: small AI models that check answers against a source. They could be added later as an optional local add-on, but would make the file tens of MB, so they're left out to keep this standalone.
